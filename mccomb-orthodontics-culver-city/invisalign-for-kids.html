How many hours per day does my child need to wear the aligners?

20 to 22 hours per day. The aligners come out for eating, drinking anything other than water, and brushing. They stay in during sleep.

What age should my child have their first orthodontic evaluation?

The American Association of Orthodontists recommends age 7. McComb Orthodontics sees most children for a first evaluation between 7 and 9. At that age, a child typically has a mix of baby teeth and incoming permanent teeth, which gives the orthodontist a clear picture of how the jaw and bite are developing and whether any intervention is needed.

The evaluation includes a panoramic X-ray, photos, and a 3D scan. It is free. Most children who come in at 7 or 8 do not need treatment yet. For those children, McComb places them in a complimentary Observation Program with check-ins every 8 to 12 months until their adult teeth come in.

Does my child actually need Phase 1 treatment, or is every orthodontist recommending it?

About one in five children who come in for an early evaluation actually need Phase 1 treatment. The other four are monitored and treated comprehensively when their adult teeth come in.

Some orthodontic practices treat at a much higher rate. McComb treats only when there is a clinically meaningful reason to intervene early: severe crowding that puts permanent teeth at risk of becoming impacted, a crossbite that is causing or will cause jaw growth asymmetry, or a significant underbite where early intervention can guide jaw development in a way that is no longer possible once growth slows. If your child does not meet those thresholds, McComb places them on the Observation Program. No treatment is recommended without a specific clinical reason.

What is Invisalign First?

Invisalign First is a clear aligner system designed specifically for children ages 6 to 10 who are in the mixed dentition phase, meaning they still have a combination of baby teeth and incoming permanent teeth. It is distinct from standard Invisalign in that the aligners accommodate teeth that are still erupting and are designed to guide arch development and create space for permanent teeth to come in correctly.

At McComb Orthodontics, every Invisalign First case is planned individually by Dr. McComb. The auto-generated treatment plan produced by Invisalign’s software is a starting point, not a prescription. Every tooth movement is programmed manually, including deliberate over-correction to account for the way bone and tissue respond to force.

What conditions does Invisalign First treat?

Invisalign First treats crowding, spacing, narrow arches, crossbites, overbites, underbites, and protrusion in children with mixed dentition. The cases McComb prioritizes for early intervention are those where waiting would make treatment more complex or more invasive: crossbites that are causing growth asymmetry, severe crowding where permanent canines are at risk of becoming impacted, and underbites where early jaw guidance is still possible.

What is the difference between Invisalign First and a palate expander?

This is the comparison most parents are trying to understand. A fixed palate expander (rapid palatal expander or RPE) is bonded to the upper molars and uses a screw mechanism to gradually widen the upper jaw. It is not removable. Parents or the child turn a key to activate it daily or weekly. It can achieve significant expansion reliably regardless of compliance, because the child cannot take it out.

Invisalign First can also expand the arch, but it works by applying gentle pressure through the aligners. The expansion is typically more gradual and depends on consistent wear. For cases where the primary goal is significant arch widening, a fixed expander may be more predictable. For cases where crowding, tooth alignment, and mild to moderate expansion are all part of the plan, Invisalign First often accomplishes the same goals without the bulk and discomfort of a fixed appliance.

At McComb, most Phase 1 cases that would previously have required a traditional expander can now be treated with Invisalign First. Whether that is the right choice for a specific child depends on how much expansion is needed and how realistic consistent wear is for that child. Dr. McComb will tell you directly which approach is more likely to produce the result you need.

My child’s pediatric dentist says to wait. My orthodontist says to start now. Who is right?

Both positions can be correct depending on the specific clinical situation. Pediatric dentists are not orthodontic specialists and sometimes recommend waiting as a general precaution, especially for cases that look like they might self-correct. Orthodontists who see early treatment cases regularly are better positioned to identify which cases will self-correct and which will become more complex if left alone.

The clearest way to resolve the disagreement is to ask each provider for the specific clinical reason for their recommendation. "Your child might be fine" is not a clinical rationale. "The upper canines are tracking toward impaction and early space creation reduces the probability of surgical exposure at age 13" is. If the orthodontist can give you a specific risk with a specific consequence of waiting, that deserves serious weight even if the dentist is taking a conservative position.

Will a 7 or 8 year old actually keep the aligners in?

This is the question most parents expect to be a problem, and in practice it often is not. Children ages 7 to 10 are among McComb’s most compliant Invisalign patients. Younger children in this age group tend to be more rule-following and less socially self-conscious about the process than teenagers. They are also more responsive to parental oversight, making the monitoring conversation easier.

That said, compliance in young children is a family effort, not just the child’s. Parents who build the routine into the daily structure early, keeping a case at the table, putting aligners back in immediately after meals, and treating it as a non-negotiable habit rather than a daily negotiation, tend to have the most success.

What if my child refuses to wear the aligners?

If a child consistently will not wear the aligners, McComb will discuss switching to fixed appliances. For Phase 1 cases, that typically means braces, an expander, or a combination depending on what the case requires. The goal is the clinical outcome, not a specific appliance. Switching mid-treatment is not ideal, but it is far better than continuing with an appliance that is not being worn.

This is also a reason to have a candid conversation at your consultation about your child’s specific temperament and daily routines before committing to Invisalign First. For some children, fixed appliances that do not require active compliance produce more reliable results.

How long does Phase 1 treatment take?

Most Phase 1 cases at McComb take 9 to 15 months. Some straightforward cases finish in 6 months. More complex expansion or bite correction cases can run to 18 months.

Some providers advertise Phase 1 timelines of a few months with minimal daily wear. Those timelines apply to the simplest cases with the least amount of movement needed. The majority of children who genuinely need Phase 1 intervention, those with meaningful crowding, expansion needs, or bite issues, require consistent 20 to 22 hour daily wear and a realistic treatment window to get a complete result. The timeline your orthodontist gives you at your first visit assumes full compliance. The biggest variable in how long treatment actually takes is how consistently the aligners are worn.

After Phase 1, the child transitions to a retainer and enters the Observation Program until adult teeth are fully in and Phase 2 can begin.

Will my child need Phase 2 treatment after this?

Approximately 95 % of children who complete Phase 1 go on to need Phase 2 comprehensive treatment. This is by design, not a sign that Phase 1 failed. Phase 1 has a functional goal: creating room for adult teeth to come in without becoming impacted, guiding jaw growth, or correcting a bite problem that would worsen if left alone. It is not cosmetic and it is not intended to finish everything.

Phase 2 begins when all permanent teeth are in, typically between ages 11 and 14, and focuses on fine-tuning tooth position and bite alignment. It is a full comprehensive treatment and is priced separately from Phase 1.

What does Phase 1 actually fix, and why doesn’t it finish everything?

Phase 1 addresses problems that are structural and time-sensitive: creating space in a jaw that does not have enough room, correcting a crossbite before it causes uneven jaw growth, or guiding an underbite while the jaw is still responsive to appliance forces. These are problems where timing matters and waiting makes them harder or impossible to correct without surgery.
